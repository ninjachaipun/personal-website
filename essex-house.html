Underwriting Judgment

Key Considerations & Conclusion

Essex House prices at a 3.93% cap rate — core-asset pricing — but a large share of that valuation depends on marking below-market rents up to market, and I can't verify the rent roll, the timeline, or the NOI behind any of it.

  • The seller's 57% upside claim isn't what this model uses: without an actual rent roll, I can't confirm current rents against market, or how long it would take to close that gap. Rather than take the seller's number at face value, this model assumes a more conservative 30% upside, reached by Year 3.
  • Parking revenue needs no new investment: the model assumes income from all 85 existing parking spots — the lot is already built, so there's no construction or improvement cost to fund it.
  • Laundry is owner-operated, not a vendor lease: the building offers on-site laundry with no third-party vendor involved, so the property keeps the full revenue rather than a share. The model assumes each tenant runs two loads a week.
  • Debt coverage is thin in Years 1 and 2: DSCR runs below 1.0x while rents sit below market, then clears 1.0x once the model assumes rents reach market in Year 3. The deal's success case is straightforward on paper — every unit re-leased at or above market on turnover — but there's little room for that not to happen on schedule.
  • Realty taxes aren't disclosed, and are modeled as monthly accruals: the brochure gives no tax figure or assessment, so the $213,050/yr used here is a rate-based estimate on an assumed assessment. Separately, taxes are booked monthly rather than as one annual payment purely to smooth cash flow — the total burden is unchanged either way.

My Take

Essex House is overvalued at the ask. It's priced at a core cap rate but doesn't behave like a core asset — a large share of its value depends on marking sub-market rents up to market, and in a rent-controlled building that spread can only be captured on turnover, which is a multi-year process, not the near-term step the pricing implies. No rent roll was provided, so I can't verify lease expiries or actual in-place rents, and the surrounding rental market is softening rather than tightening. The financing test confirms the problem: on in-place income, the deal can't cover its own debt service in the early years, and the loan required to close isn't one a lender would actually write. The upside here is real, but the seller is charging for the stabilized outcome while the buyer would carry all the risk and cost of getting there. Colliers' Q2 2026 Cap Rate Report puts Class A and B multi-family in this market trading at 4% on the low end — the 3.93% this deal demands is unwarranted. I'd pass at the ask and re-engage only with a full rent roll and audited operating statements.

Methodology

How the Model Is Built

Unit & Revenue Assumptions

Unit-level rent schedule by floor plan (beds/sq. ft.), monthly lease-up and vacancy schedules, and itemized parking and laundry income, benchmarked against the listing's in-place rents.

Debt & Cash Flow

Full monthly debt amortization schedule, operating expense assumptions (property tax, insurance, management fee, capex), and monthly-to-annual NOI and cash flow roll-ups.

Returns & Risk

Unlevered/levered IRR, NPV, and equity multiple calculated at the deal and individual-investor level, with a 9×9 two-way sensitivity grid on rent growth, discount rate, and exit cap rate.

ⓘ Market validation: the model's 4.0% exit cap rate assumption is grounded in Colliers' Q2 2026 Canada Cap Rate Report, which places Toronto multifamily cap rates in a 4.00%–4.75% range for Class A/B assets — the model uses the low end, reflecting the property's improved condition and transit-oriented location.
