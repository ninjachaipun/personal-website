Underwriting Judgment

Key Considerations

Zoning

2480 Dundas St W is classified as Industrial Commercial (IC), yet it is currently operated as a multi-residential live/work property. While this flexible use has merit, it creates potential issues for future redevelopment and could complicate the property's valuation. There is a risk that zoning restrictions may impose complications during any future sale or redevelopment. For this model, it is assumed all units will remain residential.

High Purchase Cap Rate

The purchase cap rate is slightly over 6%, significantly higher than the recommended 4.0% to 4.75% range for Toronto Class A and Class B multifamily properties. This elevated cap rate signals substantial risks the buyer assumes at purchase.

As a standalone multifamily investment, the property carries risk due to its age and the need for ongoing major capital expenditures despite recent renovations. Additionally, the zoning complexity presents a future obstacle if redevelopment becomes necessary. Compounding these concerns, most rents are on month-to-month (MTM) leases, creating vacancy and rental rate volatility.

However, when viewed as an industrial property aligned with its actual zoning, the 6% cap rate aligns perfectly with industrial property cap rates from the Q2 2026 Colliers Toronto Cap Rate Report. This interpretation reconciles the pricing with the building's Industrial Commercial classification.

The exit cap rate assumes multifamily property logic, reduced to reflect the substantial renovations, but with an additional premium applied for zoning risk.

Revenue Adjustment

Current in-place rents are above market rates. Because these rents are on MTM leases, fast tenant turnover is expected, and rents will decline to market rates quickly. However, due value-add renovations, the model believes a premium should be applied to the market prices for units. In-depth market research should conducted to ensure up to date market rates for units similar to those in this property.

DSCR Concerns

Due to the nature of the financing structure, the Debt-Service-Coverage is expected to be a non-issue. The first Bridge Loan will not require a minimum DSCR because the project is yet to be stabilized. Later in Year 2, or Month 18 of the project, when the property is stabilized the Bridge Loan is refinanced to a Permanent Loan. Upon that time, the DSCR is above the minimum of 1.25x for all years required by lenders.

The lowest DSCR is expected is 1.28x in the first year of operations, which is a slim cushion.

Tax Payments

Property taxes are modeled as monthly accruals rather than annual lump-sum payments. While taxes are paid annually in practice, this approach smooths monthly cash flows and minimizes reserve draws during high-tax months. The total tax burden remains unchanged; this is a cash management approach that enhances model clarity.

The tax estimate was recalculated from the sale brochure information. The seller's claimed tax payment of approximately $17,350 appears suspiciously low for a property with an asking price near $6 million.

Conclusion & Decision:

Poor Investment Opportunity

The brochure's disclosed taxes of about $17,000 look understated. They imply an assessed value of only $1.43M, about 24% of the asking price. A reassessment at purchase would raise taxes by about $37,000 a year and cut NOI from the stated $368,000 to about $331,000. At the quoted 6.15% cap rate, that NOI supports a value of about $5.38M, so the $5.99M asking price carries an 11% (~$601,000) premium.

The rent base adds risk. Nearly all leases are month-to-month, which makes renovation turnover easy but means the income is unstable over the long term. The value-add plan works only if renovated units can be re-leased on longer terms at a premium to market rents. Without renovations the outlook is worse: in-place rents are above market and falling, so turnover would push income down to market levels. The building's age (over 100 years) also means significant ongoing capital spending.

At the asking price, the deal returns a levered IRR of 8.7%, below the 10% discount rate, and a levered NPV of –$254,000. At the corrected price of $5.38M, IRR rises about 4 points to 12.8% and NPV improves by about $705,000, to +$463,000. Understated taxes, unstable above-market rents and a century-old building make this a poor investment at the current asking price.

This deal would be a more attractive opportunity with a lower acquisition price.
