Underwriting Judgment

Key Considerations & Conclusion

2480 Dundas is a smaller, more interesting deal than my first model — I actually have a documented rent roll and 100% occupancy here, but the numbers surface a financing problem that a rent roll can't fix on its own.

  • Zoning conflict — I2 IC vs. MCR: the offering brochure states the property is zoned I2 IC (industrial); the public LoopNet listing states MCR (mixed-use commercial-residential). I haven't been able to reconcile these, and it matters — it affects whether the current live/work use, and any future intensification, is actually permitted as-of-right.
  • Negative leverage in the base case: this model's levered IRR (7.4%) is actually lower than its unlevered IRR (7.6%) — the 5.5% cost of debt is eating into returns rather than amplifying them. Year 1 debt service coverage comes out to 0.92x, meaning the property doesn't cover its own debt service in year 1 as modeled. That's usually a hard stop for a lender, not just a modelling footnote.
  • Thin margin for error: base-case levered NPV is $169K on a $2.52M equity check. The sensitivity table below shows this turning negative under a fairly modest combination of a higher discount rate and a softer exit cap rate — there isn't much room here to be wrong.
  • Property tax is frozen well below market: Ontario assessments haven't moved since Jan 1, 2016. Stated 2025 taxes imply an assessed value of roughly $728K against a purchase price near $6M — when Toronto eventually reassesses, taxes could rise materially, on a timeline nobody controls.
  • Development upside depends on other people's projects: the brochure leans on two nearby redevelopments (Bloor/Dundas Choice, 1540–1550 Bloor) to make the case for future intensification. Neither is confirmed, and industrial-zone rental replacement rules would apply to any conversion of the current 14 units.
  • 108 years old with only partial modernization: the 2016 renovation covered the envelope, interiors, and some systems, but electrical and risers are likely original or 1980s-era. The operating expense assumptions in this model may understate what a full systems refresh eventually costs.

My Take

On paper this clears a bar — positive IRR, positive NPV, fourteen fully-occupied units with a documented rent roll. But the return is thin, leverage is working against the deal rather than for it, and the year 1 debt coverage ratio alone would probably keep this from getting financed the way I've modeled it. I'd treat this less as a stabilized income buy and more as a development-optionality play, which means the real underwriting question isn't in this spreadsheet — it's in the two zoning questions (I2 IC vs. MCR, and what the neighboring redevelopments actually do) that decide whether there's a second act here at all.

Methodology

How the Model Is Built

Unit & Revenue Assumptions

Unit-level rent roll by floor plan, current market rents from the brochure's own rent schedule, and a rooftop signage lease as the model's first itemized ancillary income line.

Debt & Cash Flow

Full monthly debt amortization schedule, operating expense assumptions (property tax, insurance, management fee, capex), and monthly-to-annual NOI and cash flow roll-ups.

Returns & Risk

Unlevered/levered IRR, NPV, and equity multiple, with a 9×9 two-way sensitivity grid on rent growth, discount rate, and exit cap rate — finer-grained than the 5×5 grid in my first model.

ⓘ Market validation: the model's 5.0% exit cap rate assumption reflects a reduced cap rate from significant renovations and surrounding-area improvement, but is still held relatively high given the building's age and unresolved zoning question — broadly consistent with Colliers' Q2 2026 Canada Cap Rate Report range for Toronto multifamily.
